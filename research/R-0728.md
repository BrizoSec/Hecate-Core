## 1. System Research: How It Works

### Summary
The SilkParasite infrastructure utilizes SpiceRAT command-and-control servers to target energy and government entities in Central Asia, operating through a cluster of hosts linked by shared TLS certificates and cloned web content. This activity, potentially attributed to China-nexus actors, relies on impersonation of legitimate regional organizations to facilitate espionage and data exfiltration.

### Key Findings
- A TLS certificate impersonating Uzbekistan's state railway authority was issued by TLC, a Chinese state-affiliated certificate authority, establishing a link between the infrastructure and specific geopolitical entities.
- The C2 servers host a byte-for-byte identical copy of the RTX Corporation (formerly Raytheon) homepage, serving as a unique detection signature that linked 13 distinct hosts across multiple European hosting providers.
- Passive DNS analysis reveals subdomain infrastructure dating back to mid-2022, indicating at least four years of ongoing operations by the SilkParasite group.
- The infrastructure spoofs domains associated with named Central Asian government and energy entities, including Türkmengaz, the Galkynysh gas field, Tojiktelecom, and Turkmenistan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs.

## 2. Adversary Tradecraft: Attack Techniques

### Summary
The SilkParasite adversary cluster utilizes shared infrastructure, including cloned corporate webpages and TLS certificates issued by Chinese state-affiliated authorities, to support SpiceRAT command and control operations targeting energy and government sectors in Central Asia. This infrastructure reveals a long-standing campaign dating back to mid-2022, linking multiple malware families such as NodeEdgeRAT and NomadRAT through common registration and certificate artifacts.

### Key Findings
- Shared TLS certificates issued by TLC, a Chinese state-affiliated certificate authority, impersonate entities like Uzbekistan's railway authority and link SpiceRAT servers to other malware families including NodeEdgeRAT and BloodAlchemy.
- Infrastructure analysis identified 13 distinct server IPs associated with the cluster via a byte-for-byte identical cloned copy of RTX Corporation's homepage, which served as a detection signature for the group.
- Domains actively spoof Central Asian government and energy entities, including Türkmengaz, the Galkynysh gas field, Tojiktelecom, and Turkmenistan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs.
- Passive DNS data indicates subdomain infrastructure dating to mid-2022, suggesting the SilkParasite campaign has been ongoing for at least four years prior to the reported 2026 activity window.
- Hunt.io detected SpiceRAT servers in March 2026 across multiple European hosting providers, with some hosts predating Bitdefender's official SilkParasite report and sharing hostnames like ns2.asiainfo.it[.]com.

## 5. Research Synthesis

### Executive Summary
SilkParasite operators maintain a persistent, multi-family malware infrastructure (SpiceRAT, NodeEdgeRAT, NomadRAT) spanning at least four years, leveraging shared TLS certificates issued by Chinese state-affiliated CAs and byte-for-byte identical cloned webpages as unique detection signatures. This cluster actively impersonates Central Asian government and energy entities to facilitate espionage against targeted sectors.

### Recommended Hypothesis
> Adversaries use shared TLS certificates from Chinese state-affiliated CAs and cloned corporate webpages as infrastructure fingerprints to support long-term SpiceRAT command-and-control operations targeting Central Asian government and energy sectors.

### Gaps Identified
- Passive DNS and certificate transparency logs are blind to the internal payload delivery mechanisms, leaving a visibility gap for initial access vectors and lateral movement techniques that establish these C2 connections.
- Traditional network monitoring often fails to correlate disparate hosting providers and autonomous systems when the attacker utilizes dynamic IP rotation across multiple reseller brands, obscuring the link between geographically distributed nodes.
