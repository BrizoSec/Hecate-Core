## 1. System Research: How It Works

### Summary
Elastic SIEM utilizes a monthly automated telemetry pipeline to score 1,781 prebuilt detection rules across noise, performance, threat, and profile dimensions using real-world fleet data. This system dynamically assigns tags such as 'Noise: Low' or 'Performance: Fast' based on deterministic metrics like alert volume and execution time, helping security teams prioritize rule deployment.

### Key Findings
- The scoring pipeline is entirely deterministic for Noise, Performance, and Profile tags, using inputs like analytics telemetry (alert volume, distinct cluster counts), execution metrics (average, p95, max times in milliseconds), and rule metadata (TOML), with LLMs used only as a fallback for Threat classification gaps.
- Noise classification relies on 'global_noise' (mean alerts per distinct environment) and platform-specific percentiles, holding new rules in 'Noise: Unknown' for 60 days to distinguish between genuinely low-noise rules and those simply not yet widely enabled.
- Performance tags use absolute thresholds rather than percentiles, where 'Fast' requires an average execution time of ≤400ms, p95 <1s, no 30s+ executions, and sampling from at least five distinct environments.
- The Profile tag synthesizes severity, noise, performance, and threat coverage into a score, with 'Recommended' requiring ≥7 points plus hard gates against high noise or low severity, while 'Threat' tags carry the highest weight (+4) for covering actively tracked attack techniques.
- The automated monthly process generates a draft pull request on the elastic/detection-rules GitHub repository, which is manually reviewed by the Threat Command team before merging to ensure accuracy of tag migrations.

## 2. Adversary Tradecraft: Attack Techniques

### Summary
This text does not describe an adversary abuse technique; rather, it details Elastic SIEM's automated methodology for grading its own prebuilt detection rules. The system uses monthly telemetry to score rules on noise, performance, and threat coverage to help defenders prioritize enabling high-value, low-noise detections.

### Key Findings
- Elastic SIEM uses a deterministic monthly pipeline scoring 1,781 prebuilt rules across four dimensions: Noise, Performance, Threat, and Profile.
- The 'Threat' tag identifies coverage for specific adversary techniques such as Supply Chain attacks, ClickFix, Phishing Kits, and Remote Monitoring and Management (RMM) abuse.
- Rules are categorized into 'Profile: Recommended' (385 rules) if they score 7+ points based on severity, low noise, fast performance, and threat coverage, or 'Profile: Aggressive' (296 rules) for high-noise or low-value detections.
- The scoring system deliberately avoids labeling new rules as 'Noise: Low' within their first 60 days to prevent misleading classifications before sufficient telemetry data is collected.
- LLMs are used only as a fallback for Threat classification where heuristics fail, and they are constrained to suggest only categories from an approved managed catalog; they cannot invent new threat types.

## 5. Research Synthesis

### Executive Summary
Elastic SIEM's automated grading pipeline dynamically scores detection rules using deterministic fleet telemetry to prioritize high-signal, low-noise detections for active threat coverage. While this methodology optimizes defensive efficiency by filtering out ineffective rules, it introduces a critical blind spot: attacks leveraging low-fidelity, infrequent, or non-standard TTPs may fall into 'Noise: Unknown' or 'Profile: Aggressive' categories, causing defenders to miss high-value threats due to automated suppression or lack of prioritization.

### Recommended Hypothesis
> Adversaries use low-frequency, non-standard execution techniques on under-monitored platforms to evade deterministic noise-based filtering and bypass the 'Profile: Recommended' prioritization pipeline.

### Gaps Identified
- The 60-day 'Noise: Unknown' hold period for new rules creates a temporal blind spot where novel attack vectors targeting recently deployed or updated environments may go undetected until sufficient telemetry accumulates.
- Deterministic performance thresholds (e.g., strict p95 <1s requirements) may inadvertently suppress detections for complex, multi-stage attacks that inherently require longer execution windows or higher resource utilization to manifest.

### Key Findings
- Hypothesis: Adversaries use low-frequency, non-standard execution techniques on under-monitored platforms to evade deterministic noise-based filtering and bypass the 'Profile: Recommended' prioritization pipeline.
- Gap: The 60-day 'Noise: Unknown' hold period for new rules creates a temporal blind spot where novel attack vectors targeting recently deployed or updated environments may go undetected until sufficient telemetry accumulates.
- Gap: Deterministic performance thresholds (e.g., strict p95 <1s requirements) may inadvertently suppress detections for complex, multi-stage attacks that inherently require longer execution windows or higher resource utilization to manifest.
- Focus: Target rules tagged 'Profile: Aggressive' or 'Threat' categories but currently suppressed due to high noise volumes, as these often represent active adversary tradecraft (e.g., RMM abuse, Supply Chain) that requires manual tuning rather than automated dismissal.
