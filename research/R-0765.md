## 1. System Research: How It Works

### Summary
Hangro is a North Korean state-sponsored VPN and mail relay system, derived from SoftEther technology, used by trade representatives abroad to connect back to domestic infrastructure via servers in Pyongyang and the Russian Far East. It functions as a bundled client providing VPN, IMAP/POP mail, and chat services, distributing traffic through specific IP ranges while utilizing a certificate hierarchy that contains cryptographic anomalies.

### Key Findings
- The Hangro client software is derived from SoftEther VPN, evidenced by the use of the 'NeoAdapter_VPN' hardware ID in its network driver, with metadata indicating versions dating back to 2013 and 2014.
- The system operates across a multi-national infrastructure, utilizing IP addresses in North Korean address space (e.g., 175.45.176.x) and Russian Far East networks (e.g., 188.43.136.x), with historical documentation also noting connections to Chinese address space linked to Silibank.
- The TLS certificate hierarchy for the 2024 deployment exhibits critical cryptographic failures: signatures fail verification against the issuer's public key, and the server demonstrates a mismatch between its presented public key and the private key used for handshakes on VPN/mail ports, though it does not verify these chains itself.

## 2. Adversary Tradecraft: Attack Techniques

### Summary
North Korea’s state-sponsored Hangro infrastructure, a VPN and mail relay system, has been updated with a new certificate hierarchy deployed in July 2026 that reveals extensive cross-border network assignments in China and Russia. Despite significant cryptographic anomalies where signature verification fails, the system continues to function as a critical communication channel for North Korean trade representatives abroad, leveraging legacy SoftEther-derived software components.

### Key Findings
- Hangro is a state VPN and mail product used by North Korean trade representatives in China (Jilin, Liaoning, Heilongjiang) and Russia, distributed through the Shenyang consulate at $350 per seat.
- A new certificate hierarchy went live in July 2026 on port 6006, utilizing RSA-4096 keys and a ten-year validity period, distinct from the previous EC P-384 based 2024 hierarchy.
- Investigation linked six network assignments in Chinese address space to a single registry contact associated with Silibank, alongside infrastructure in Russian Far East networks designated KPOST.
- The service exhibits severe cryptographic anomalies: signatures in the 2024 hierarchy fail verification against their public keys, and the client does not verify certificate chains, trusting only pre-installed certificates.
- Technical analysis confirms Hangro is derived from SoftEther VPN software, evidenced by the 'NeoAdapter_VPN' hardware ID in its Windows driver, with the last modification dates aligning with September 2014.
- The system supports bulk mail transfers over intermittent connections with ten-gigabyte message limits and ETRN capabilities, serving as the primary email channel between North Korean authorities and companies abroad.

## 5. Research Synthesis

### Executive Summary
North Korea's Hangro infrastructure, a SoftEther-derived VPN and mail relay system used by trade representatives abroad, has evolved with a new July 2026 certificate hierarchy revealing cross-border presence in China (linked to Silibank) and Russia. The deployment exhibits severe cryptographic anomalies where signature verification fails against public keys, yet the client bypasses chain validation by relying on pre-installed trusted certificates. Detection requires focusing on specific driver hardware IDs and non-standard TLS handshake behaviors rather than standard certificate trust checks.

### Recommended Hypothesis
> Adversaries use a cryptographically anomalous, SoftEther-derived VPN client with pre-installed self-signed certificates to maintain resilient command-and-control and mail relay channels for North Korean trade representatives across cross-border infrastructure.

### Gaps Identified
- Standard TLS inspection tools will fail to detect this traffic because the server's private key does not match its public certificate on ports 465/7443, causing handshake failures that look like noise or errors rather than malicious C2 activity.
- Current network telemetry lacks visibility into the specific 'NeoAdapter_VPN' hardware ID and driver version metadata required to distinguish Hangro's modified SoftEther components from legitimate VPN software.

### Key Findings
- Hypothesis: Adversaries use a cryptographically anomalous, SoftEther-derived VPN client with pre-installed self-signed certificates to maintain resilient command-and-control and mail relay channels for North Korean trade representatives across cross-border infrastructure.
- Gap: Standard TLS inspection tools will fail to detect this traffic because the server's private key does not match its public certificate on ports 465/7443, causing handshake failures that look like noise or errors rather than malicious C2 activity.
- Gap: Current network telemetry lacks visibility into the specific 'NeoAdapter_VPN' hardware ID and driver version metadata required to distinguish Hangro's modified SoftEther components from legitimate VPN software.
- Focus: Hunt for process execution of 'HangroVPN' and the presence of the 'NeoAdapter_VPN' driver (Hardware ID NeoAdapter_VPN) on endpoints, correlating with outbound TLS connections to known Hangro IP ranges (175.45.176.x, 188.43.136.x) and Silibank-linked Chinese subnets.
- Focus: Analyze TLS handshake logs for specific anomalies, such as successful certificate verification on port 6006 but immediate 'certificate_required' alerts or bad signature errors on ports 465/7443, indicating the use of mismatched key pairs characteristic of this infrastructure.
