## 1. System Research: How It Works

### Summary
Spring Ring is a voice phishing campaign that leverages Microsoft Teams to impersonate internal support staff, tricking victims into executing commands or downloading malware through trusted communication channels. The attack does not exploit vulnerabilities in the platform but relies on social engineering and the victim's willingness to cooperate with perceived legitimate IT requests.

### Key Findings
- Attackers initiate conversations from crafted personas that mimic official company identities within Microsoft Teams, often starting with voice calls.
- The campaign focuses on deploying PowerShell scripts or executables via remote access tools like RDP sessions after gaining initial foothold through social engineering.
- Normal network traffic may include legitimate-looking connections to Microsoft services but also suspicious outbound requests or lateral movement indicative of the attacker's presence within the network.

## 2. Adversary Tradecraft: Attack Techniques

### Summary
The Spring Ring campaign abuses Microsoft Teams by creating external accounts that impersonate internal IT support to initiate voice phishing (vishing) attacks. These attacks aim to trick employees into installing malware or remote access tools, with the ultimate goal of gaining domain-level access through NTLM relay attacks.

### Key Findings
- Attackers create crafted personas and domains within Microsoft Teams to mimic legitimate internal support units for social engineering purposes.
- Spring Ring leverages external Microsoft Teams accounts to initiate direct contact with targets, impersonating IT help desk personnel.
- The campaign does not exploit vulnerabilities in the Microsoft Teams platform itself but instead relies on trust in familiar communication channels.
- Once initiated, these attacks can progress into multi-stage intrusions without needing further exploits of the Teams system.

## 5. Research Synthesis

### Executive Summary
Spring Ring is a voice phishing campaign that leverages Microsoft Teams to deploy malware by tricking victims into executing commands or downloading malicious payloads via social engineering. The campaign focuses on deploying PowerShell scripts and Remote Access Trojans (RATs) to gain domain-level access.

### Recommended Hypothesis
> Adversaries use voice phishing in Microsoft Teams to initiate multi-stage intrusions, deploying PowerShell scripts and RATs to compromise enterprise domain controllers.

### Gaps Identified
- The current detection mechanisms may struggle with identifying social engineering tactics used to create legitimate-looking personas within Microsoft Teams that trick victims into executing malicious commands or downloading malware.
- Network traffic analysis might not effectively differentiate between benign activity and suspicious lateral movement indicative of an attacker's presence, especially when the latter mimics regular enterprise communication patterns.

### Key Findings
- Hypothesis: Adversaries use voice phishing in Microsoft Teams to initiate multi-stage intrusions, deploying PowerShell scripts and RATs to compromise enterprise domain controllers.
- Gap: The current detection mechanisms may struggle with identifying social engineering tactics used to create legitimate-looking personas within Microsoft Teams that trick victims into executing malicious commands or downloading malware.
- Gap: Network traffic analysis might not effectively differentiate between benign activity and suspicious lateral movement indicative of an attacker's presence, especially when the latter mimics regular enterprise communication patterns.
- Focus: Enhance detection capabilities for PowerShell script executions and file drops associated with known malicious artifacts to identify initial infection vectors.
- Focus: Improve network monitoring to detect anomalous outbound connections from compromised systems to command and control servers, particularly those that mimic legitimate traffic.
